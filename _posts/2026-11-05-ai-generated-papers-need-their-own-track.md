The cost of generating a research paper is falling fast. The cost of reviewing one is not.

We are already seeing the pressure. The [NeurIPS 2026 Position Paper Track](https://blog.neurips.cc/2026/06/02/ai-generated-papers-in-the-neurips-2026-position-paper-track/) announced that it would reject 178 submissions (18.4%) for breaking its AI-writing policy and ask another 123 (12.7%) to show evidence of substantial human involvement. [ICLR 2026](https://blog.iclr.cc/2025/11/19/iclr-2026-response-to-llm-generated-papers-and-reviews/) also reported concerns about AI-generated papers and reviews. These cases do not tell us how many papers were fully produced by AI. They do show that the current review system is under pressure.

At the same time, AI research agents are improving. Researchers will keep using them to run experiments, write papers, and explore ideas. I think that is worth encouraging. The question is how conferences should handle the growing volume of their work.

## A separate track

I propose that major AI conferences create a dedicated **AI-generated paper track**, with two rules.

**First, human-written and AI-generated papers should go through separate review tracks.** Authors who submit AI-generated papers should be required to personally review AI-generated papers from other groups. They can use AI tools to help, but they must take responsibility for their reviews. Conferences could assign a set number of reviews per submission, with the usual rules for conflicts of interest and review quality.

This keeps the cost of reviewing AI-generated research within the community producing it. Researchers submitting human-led work should not have to spend their review time checking a growing number of papers produced mainly by AI. A separate track would also give AI-generated research a place to develop its own standards.

**Second, deliberate attempts to avoid the track should have consequences.** If a group knowingly submits mostly AI-generated work to the main track while hiding how it was produced, a desk rejection is too weak. I would support a one-year ban on submissions from the responsible research group across major AI conferences, after a careful investigation and with a clear appeal process. An AI detector score alone should never be enough.

The rule should focus on who did the scientific work and who can stand behind its claims. Using AI tools for coding, analysis, or writing help is a normal part of research. That should remain welcome in the main track.

## Why it matters

Peer review depends on researchers giving their time to check each other's work. If generating papers becomes almost free while checking them stays expensive, a few groups can place a large cost on the whole community.

An AI-generated paper track would make room for a promising form of research while asking its authors to do their share of the reviewing. It would protect the time researchers contribute to the main conference tracks.

**If AI can scale scientific production, we also need to scale scientific verification.**
